Data & Meaning

Clean records, shared definitions, and sound turned into context — the ground that decides whether business intelligence deserves the name.

Bad data bills everyone, twice

Poor data quality charges an organisation two times: once when the bad record causes a wrong decision, a failed delivery, or a support call, and again when teams lose trust in every number around the bad one and start keeping private spreadsheets. Models make the bill arrive faster. An agent reading dirty records does not slow down to doubt them; the agent acts, fluently and at scale, on whatever the records say. That is why the order of operations in our studio never changes: clean the data before automating anything. Automation amplifies whatever already exists. Clean data becomes faster truth; dirty data becomes faster fiction, with better formatting.

What cleaning actually means

Cleaning is not a script run once before a project starts. Cleaning means naming the fields that matter, deciding what a valid value looks like for each one, and building checks that catch the invalid ones at the door rather than in the quarterly report. Duplicates get merged under rules a human approved. Missing values get a policy: estimate, flag, or refuse, chosen per field, never by accident. Formats converge until one customer is one record and one date means one thing. The work continues after launch, because data decays: people change jobs, companies move, products retire. A dataset cleaned in January is a rumour by December unless the checks keep running. Treating quality as a standing process rather than a project phase is the single habit that separates data teams whose numbers get believed from teams whose numbers get footnotes.

A correct metric is only step one

Even perfect records can produce perfect confusion. When two departments define revenue differently, both calculations can be internally correct and jointly useless. Business intelligence holds up only when meaning is governed alongside quality: one semantic layer where definitions live, rules turn phrases like Premier Customer into executable and versioned tests, and every dashboard and agent reads from the same statement of what words mean. The sequence matters. Quality first, so the records deserve trust; definitions second, so the reports deserve agreement; judgment last, built on ground that no longer shifts. Skipping the second step is why so many BI programmes deliver beautiful charts and recurring arguments.

Sound becomes context

Meaning also hides outside tables entirely. Meetings, calls, and field recordings carry decisions, commitments, and complaints that never reach a database, and until recently the only way in was a transcript chopped into fragments. Transcription models now hold a full hour of recorded audio inside a single context of 65,536 tokens, which changes what the material can do: an hour of conversation becomes one context, searchable and reason-able as a whole rather than as confetti. Sound becomes first-class context, joinable with the structured record. A support organisation can ask what customers actually said this month across every call, and the answer can carry the shape of the conversations, not only keywords. The same quality rules apply, doubled: recordings carry names and personal detail, so permission and retention decisions belong in the design from the first day.

BI that holds up

Business intelligence earns the name when three conditions hold at once. The records are clean and stay clean under standing checks. The definitions are shared, versioned, and applied identically by dashboards and agents alike. And the sources include the unstructured material where the business actually speaks: documents, messages, and sound, converted into context with care. Under those conditions, a dashboard stops being a decoration and becomes an instrument, and an agent reading the same governed ground gives answers that match the reports the leadership team already trusts. Data and meaning are one subject, not two. The records supply the facts; the meaning decides what the facts are about; and the quality of both decides whether intelligence is a property of the system or only a word in the programme title.

The audit habit

Quality survives through habit, not through ceremonies. A weekly audit can be small: sample fifty records across the fields that matter, score each record against the written rules, and publish the count where the data team cannot avoid seeing the count. Defects get owners and deadlines the way incidents do. A monthly review asks which rules caught the most damage and which new failure shape appeared, then updates the checks at the door. None of the ritual requires new software; a query, a spreadsheet, and a standing appointment cover the mechanics. What the ritual changes is attention. Data quality decays in the dark, quietly, between projects. A small, regular light keeps the decay visible while repair stays cheap, and the trust of everyone downstream, analysts, dashboards, and agents alike, rests on exactly that visibility.
